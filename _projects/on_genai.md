I do not use generative artificial intelligence[^1] in any capacity and discourage students from outsourcing learning and effort to these tools, as well. It is a _privilege_ to be able to do science, and in addition to my disinclination to give up doing the job I've worked so hard to pursue or contribute to my own future obsolescence by training, and validating the use of, these algorithms for the thought, writing, or analysis that encompass the act of doing science, there are plenty of studies that point specifically to the harm in the widespread and uncritical adoption of generative AI.

I share a non-comprehensive list of results and supporting literature here, though I strongly encourage anyone who is questioning their use of AI to read more for themselves and seek out additional results.

There are a couple of other points I'll add:
- Critically, when an instructor asks a student to do something, it's not because that thing _needs_ to be done for the instructor. Instructors don't benefit from reading 20 student papers on the same topic; instead, students benefit from having written that paper. The goal of assignments (in education or in research) is for the student to actually undertake the work of completing the assigned task. That effort is what makes the exercise worthwhile. Using generative AI to offload work -- and I'd argue this is true even for professional scientists whose jobs center on creativity, discovery, and, best of all, getting paid to think big thoughts -- is like using a forklift to pick up weights at the gym, then wondering why you aren't getting any stronger. 
- I've seen people describe the behavior of generative AI companies as "inherently antisocial" in the context of polluting the scientific literature with findings that will require human time and effort to validate while also breaking the chain of knowledge we all rely on to put results in context. I strongly agree with this sentiment, and would similarly note that it breaks the rules of common courtesy too: if someone hasn't viewed it as worth their time to write a message or a paper, why should I view it as worth my time to read it, and why does the person who is signing their name to those words feel my time is worth so much less than theirs?


LLMs also reinforce biases (and hallucinate results, though people claim this is no longer an issue) -- including jumping the line in who gets credit, when we still don't recognize the work of many real scientists -- and make it harder to establish oneself as a researcher given the (increasingly understandable) distrust of newcomers in a field given the "lower" barrier to entry. [^1]: I make the distinction here between generative artificial intelligence, like large language models, and the machine learning algorithms that scientists have been using for decades. "AI" has become a catch-all term both in popular media and in academia, which obfuscates the differences in methodology between tools and ascribes, in the public imagination at least, scientific discovery and advancement to generative AI that was actually the result of intentional effort by highly trained _human_ scientists.
